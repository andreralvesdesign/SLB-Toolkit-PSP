Grief: being there
through loss

Grief is a normal human response to the loss of a significant person. It means adapting to a new reality and learning to live in a world where the person who died is no longer present. At Benfica, the loss may be that of a colleague, a family member or someone dear to the Benfica community; when grief is collective, it is also lived in the workplace.

7 screens

What may be happening?

Every grieving process is unique

It is influenced by factors such as the relationship with the person who died, the circumstances of the death, life history and the support network available. It can show up through different reactions, especially in the early stages.

Emotional

  • Deep sadness
  • Anger
  • Guilt
  • Anxiety
  • A feeling of helplessness or emptiness

Physical

  • Tightness in the chest or throat
  • Shortness of breath
  • Muscle weakness
  • Hypersensitivity to noise
  • A feeling of emptiness in the stomach

Cognitive

  • Mental confusion
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Persistent thoughts about the person who has died
  • A sense of presence or transient auditory/visual experiences related to the person who has died

Behavioural

  • Changes in sleep and appetite
  • Social isolation
  • Frequent crying
  • Agitation
  • Looking for objects or memories associated with the person

What may be happening?

The process may involve

  1. 1
    Accepting the reality of the loss
  2. 2
    Processing the emotional pain
  3. 3
    Adapting to a new routine
  4. 4
    Preserving a symbolic bond with the person who has gone, bringing their presence into memory and affection while carrying on living

What to do?

What to do?

The intervention should focus on active listening, emotional validation and support for gradual adaptation to the loss.

  1. 1
    Facilitate acceptance of the loss: Allow the person to talk about the death, the funeral or the circumstances of the loss, helping to integrate the reality of what has happened.
  2. 2
    Help with identifying emotions: Encourage the expression of difficult emotions, such as guilt, anger or sadness, without judgement.
  3. 3
    Support the reorganisation of daily life: Help with adapting to new responsibilities, decisions or routines.
  4. 4
    Use symbolic elements: Photographs, letters, rituals or memories can facilitate emotional expression and meaningful connection.
  5. 5
    Validate the legacy of the person who has died: Promote ways of honouring the story and meaning of the person who has been lost.

What to avoid?

What to avoid?

  1. Using clichés or minimising the pain: Avoid phrases such as “You have to be strong”, “Time heals everything”, “I know exactly how you feel”.
  2. Expecting rigid stages: Grief does not follow a linear sequence or the same one for everyone.
  3. Encouraging impulsive decisions: Avoid major life changes immediately after the loss.
  4. Pathologising initial reactions: Intense crying, frequent dreams or a sense of the deceased’s presence can be part of the normal grieving process.
  5. Assuming everyone needs therapy: Many people adapt with informal support and relational support.

When to refer?

When to refer?

Referral to psychological support should be considered when:

  • !The distress remains intense and persistent for a long period
  • !There is a significant inability to resume personal, social or professional functioning
  • !Severe symptoms of anxiety, depression or substance abuse appear
  • !The person remains “stuck” in the loss, unable to integrate the absence
  • !There are persistent physical symptoms with no medical explanation
  • !There are self-harming behaviours, suicidal ideation or risk behaviours
