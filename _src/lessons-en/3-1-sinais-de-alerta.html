Warning signs

Distress does not always show itself clearly

It often shows up through small changes in mood, body, thinking or relationships. It may begin with signs such as less patience, persistent tiredness, difficulty concentrating, a greater wish to withdraw or a feeling of not being able to stop.

Recognising early signs matters because people do not experience distress in the same way. Some become quieter or more distant; others, more irritable or reactive. In some people the impact shows mainly in the body, through tension, aches or fatigue; in others, it appears more in their thoughts, such as difficulty concentrating, repetitive thoughts or increased self-criticism.

More than identifying isolated symptoms, what matters is recognising changes in your own functioning. Simple questions can help:

  • “Am I different from how I usually am?”
  • “Do I feel balanced, or am I just getting by?”
  • “Are these changes passing, or do they persist over time?”

What I feel inside

Emotional signs

The emotional impact can show up in very different ways, not just as sadness or anxiety. You may notice:

  • Feeling constantly “on alert” or tense
  • Persistent sadness or difficulty feeling pleasure
  • Getting irritated easily, impatience or sharper replies
  • Feeling empty, apathetic or “emotionally switched off”
  • Frequent guilt, a feeling of failing or not being enough
  • Emotional ups and downs that are more intense than usual
  • Feeling emotionally “overwhelmed”
  • Difficulty identifying or naming what you are feeling
Reflection questions

“Have I been reacting emotionally differently from usual?”

“Do I feel more… or less than is normal for me?”

What the body is expressing

Physical signs

Some people say “I’m fine”, but the body shows the opposite. The body often expresses what we have not yet been able to put into words. You may notice:

  • Constant tiredness or a feeling of “drained energy”
  • Difficulty sleeping or sleep that does not restore you
  • Frequent headaches or a feeling of pressure
  • Muscle tension (shoulders, neck, jaw)
  • Changes in appetite (increase or decrease)
  • Digestive problems or stomach discomfort
  • Tightness in the chest or shortness of breath
  • Physical agitation (restlessness) or, on the contrary, slowing down
Reflection questions

“Has my body been giving signs that I have been ignoring?”

“Do I feel physically different from my normal?”

How I think and organise information

Cognitive signs

Distress also affects the way we think and interpret the world. You may notice:

  • Difficulty concentrating or staying focused
  • More frequent forgetfulness
  • Racing thoughts or a feeling of a “full mind”
  • Repetitive or intrusive thoughts
  • A tendency towards negative or catastrophic scenarios
  • Difficulty making decisions (even small ones)
  • Intense self-criticism or a negative inner dialogue
  • A feeling of confusion or lack of mental clarity
Reflection questions

“Do I find it hard to stop thinking?”

“Have my thoughts been more critical or negative?”

What I do or stop doing

Behavioural signs

Changes in behaviour are often among the most visible signs. Some people switch off and avoid, others speed up and do not stop. You may notice:

  • Isolation or withdrawal from colleagues and relationships
  • Less involvement in tasks or loss of motivation
  • Procrastination or avoiding responsibilities
  • More mistakes or difficulty maintaining performance
  • More impulsive reactions or more conflicts
  • Working too much to avoid stopping (hyperfunctioning)
  • Doing less of the activities that used to bring pleasure
  • Difficulty setting limits
Reflection questions

“Am I avoiding things that I used to do easily?”

“Am I doing too much… or too little?”

How I relate to others

Relational signs

Distress is also reflected in how we connect with people. You may notice:

  • Less patience or more irritation with colleagues
  • Difficulty communicating clearly
  • A feeling of being misunderstood or distant
  • Avoiding conversations or interactions
  • A greater need for validation or reassurance
  • More frequent or more intense conflicts
Reflection questions

“Have my relationships been affected?”

“Am I more distant… or more reactive with others?”

Putting it together

Looking at the overall pattern

More than isolated signs, what matters most is to observe:

  • FrequencyDoes it happen often?
  • IntensityIs it stronger than usual?
  • DurationHas it lasted over time?
  • ImpactIs it interfering with how you function?

This way of looking is not meant to alarm or to pathologise normal reactions to stress, but to raise awareness and allow a more timely and appropriate response.

The sooner a person recognises that something is not right, the greater their ability to mobilise resources and look after themselves effectively.

When to ask for support?

Asking for support is a form of self-care and not a sign of weakness.

Although it is natural to go through periods of greater stress, it is important to recognise when the distress becomes persistent, more intense or starts to interfere with how you function.

  • Emotional
  • Physical
  • Cognitive
  • Behavioural
  • Relational

It may be important to seek support when you recognise several signs in these areas.

When to ask for support?

An important sign: persistence and impact

More than a single sign, it is important to observe:

  1. 1whether these changes persist over several weeks;
  2. 2whether they are getting more intense;
  3. 3and whether they are starting to interfere with your functioning, wellbeing or relationships.

Seeking help in good time can reduce the impact of distress and make it easier to return to greater balance and wellbeing.

If you recognise several of these signs

It may be an indication that you need additional support, whether from people you trust or from professionals.
