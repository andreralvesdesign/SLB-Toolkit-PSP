Module 5 · Sensitive Situations

Supporting a member or fan in distress

A member, fan or visitor in emotional distress may be more sensitive, more reactive or find it harder to express their needs in an organised way. This can happen at the Stadium gates, at the ticket office, in the store, at the Museum or on the phone, and on a matchday the emotion of the result can amplify what the person is already carrying. The aim is not to do psychotherapy, but to offer a human, safe and empathetic response within the professional role.

6 screens

What may be happening?

Emotionally demanding situations

The member, fan or visitor may be going through emotionally demanding personal or professional situations, and may show:

  • emotional hypersensitivity
  • anxiety, sadness or despair
  • irritability or anger
  • frustration with the service (tickets, membership fees, queues, access)
  • intense emotional responses
  • a need to be heard, understood and validated

What to do?

What to do?

  1. 1
    Show availability: Listen actively and respectfully, showing genuine interest in what the person is communicating.
  2. 2
    Keep a calm, empathetic posture: Speak in a quiet, predictable and non-defensive way.
  3. 3
    Validate the distress: Acknowledge that the person’s experience may be difficult or emotionally intense. “I understand that this situation must be very difficult for you.”
  4. 4
    Clarify needs: Use open, non-intrusive questions to understand what happened, what the person needs and how they can be helped at that moment.
  5. 5
    Help with problem-solving: Whenever possible, identify practical solutions, clarify next steps, reduce uncertainty.
  6. 6
    Manage expectations: Be transparent and realistic and avoid promising what you cannot guarantee.
  7. 7
    Use stabilisation strategies: If the person is very dysregulated: encourage slow breathing, use grounding, help them bring their attention back to the present.

What to avoid?

What to avoid?

  1. Trying to “fix” the distress straight away: The pressure to make the person feel better quickly can reduce the capacity for genuine listening.
  2. Invalidating or minimising: Avoid phrases such as “It’s not that bad”, “You need to calm down”.
  3. Assigning blame: Do not imply that the person’s distress is “their own fault”.
  4. Judging or labelling: Focus on the person’s need and distress, and not on what is “wrong” with them.
  5. Getting into a defensive confrontation: Avoid responding to the emotion with irritation, sarcasm or excessive rigidity.
