Part A · Identifying signs in the team

The aim is not to diagnose, but to recognise relevant changes

Early identification of signs of possible psychological distress is a core leadership skill. Signs should be analysed against the person’s usual pattern and the context in which they occur.

The central questionIs this consistent with this person’s usual functioning, or does it represent a significant change?

A single sign is rarely enough to justify immediate concern. What matters most is observing patterns that are persistent, intense or that affect professional and relational functioning.

Observing with a method

Observing has an order

Identifying signs starts with observing. Before interpreting or acting, the leader looks at three things, always in this sequence:

Is there an immediate risk to the person or to others?

E.g. signs of crisis, intense dysregulation, signs of risk.

If so, the priority stops being “identifying signs” and becomes ensuring safety and activating support.

Is there something practical that needs a response now?

E.g. acute overload, obvious exhaustion, a situation that is escalating.

What changes are there from the person’s usual functioning?

Key idea: observing is not monitoring or diagnosing. It is noticing relevant changes, with safety always coming first.

Putting the signs together

A single sign rarely justifies immediate intervention

Concern increases when there is a consistent and continuing pattern. Criteria for greater attention:

  • PersistenceThe signs continue over time.
  • IntensityThe changes become more marked.
  • ImpactThere is an effect on performance, functioning or relationships.
  • AccumulationSeveral signs appear at the same time.

The greater the convergence of these factors, the greater the need for active monitoring and, where appropriate, an approach from leadership.

Why signs vary
