Panic attacks

A panic attack is a sudden episode of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within a few minutes. Although the symptoms can be very frightening, a panic attack is not dangerous and corresponds to an intense activation of the body’s alarm system.

What may be happening?

The body goes into “fight or flight”

During a panic attack, the body goes into a state of “fight or flight”, intensely activating the sympathetic nervous system.

Physical symptoms

  • Palpitations or a racing heartbeat
  • A feeling of breathlessness or choking
  • Dizziness or feeling faint
  • Trembling
  • Sweating
  • Nausea
  • Muscle stiffness
  • Tingling

Cognitive and emotional symptoms

  • Intense fear of losing control
  • Fear of going mad or dying
  • A feeling of strangeness or unreality (derealisation)
  • A feeling of being “outside the body” (depersonalisation)

What may be happening?

The panic cycle and anticipatory anxiety

The panic cycle

  1. 1
    Normal physical sensations of anxiety
  2. 2
    They are interpreted as signs of serious danger“I’m going to die”, “I’m having a heart attack”, “I’m going mad”
  3. 3
    The sense of threat increasesThe body becomes even more activated and the physical symptoms intensify.

And the cycle starts again: a panic cycle is created.

Anticipatory anxiety

After a panic attack, a persistent fear of experiencing the symptoms again may appear.

“What if it happens again?”, “What if I faint in front of everyone?”

This can increase bodily hypervigilance and avoidance behaviours.

What to do?

What to do?

The priority is to help the person feel safe and gradually regulate their nervous system.

  1. 1
    Normalise and validate: Explain that the symptoms are an intense anxiety response and that they will ease with time.
  2. 2
    Help with breathing: Use slow, diaphragmatic breathing to reduce hyperventilation and physical tension.
  3. 3
    Promote grounding: Help the person focus on the present moment through the senses: identify objects around them, listen to sounds, feel their feet on the floor.
  4. 4
    Encourage compassionate self-talk: Encourage phrases such as “this is anxiety”, “I’ve been through this before”, “the symptoms will pass”.
  5. 5
    Reduce the sense of danger: Speak in a calm, simple and predictable way.
  6. 6
    Stay in place, if possible: Avoiding an immediate escape from the situation can help the brain relearn that the context is safe.

What to avoid?

What to avoid?

  1. Minimising or ridiculing: Avoid phrases such as “Calm down, it’s nothing”, “You’re exaggerating”.
  2. Feeding catastrophic interpretations: Do not reinforce the idea that the person is in imminent danger without a medical assessment.
  3. Pressing them to “get a grip”: Trying to force calm quickly can increase anxiety and shame.
  4. Encouraging excessive avoidance: Leaving all situations associated with panic immediately can reinforce future fear.
  5. Fighting the anxiety: Trying to eliminate the symptoms completely or “not think about it” tends to increase focus and distress.

When to refer?

When to refer?

Referral for medical and/or psychological assessment should be considered when:

  • !It is the first episode of panic
  • !There are doubts about a medical cause for the symptoms
  • !The attacks become frequent or persistent
  • !There is a significant impact on work or daily life
  • !The person starts avoiding meetings, presentations or places for fear of another attack
  • !Constant anticipatory anxiety appears
  • !Alcohol, sedatives or other substances are being used to cope with the symptoms
  • !Regulation strategies are no longer enough

Role play 5

Some situations call for a more immediate response. A panic attack gives no warning – it happens suddenly and can be very frightening for the person experiencing it and for those next to them. The role of the person offering support is not to diagnose or solve, but to create safety and help the person regulate.
